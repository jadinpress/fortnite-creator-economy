# Fortnite's Creator Economy: Money vs. Attention

Fortnite's UEFN creator program pays real money to hundreds of thousands of map-makers, but Epic
doesn't publish who gets how much. A community-run tracker, fortnite.gg, does — a full-population
breakdown of creator earnings across 75,854 creators, snapshotted here alongside two attention
leaderboards (creator engagement and map popularity) from the same site.

The framing: if you were putting a creator-sponsorship or partnership budget against this
platform, would chasing the biggest audience get you to the same creators as chasing the most
money?

**The money is concentrated: Gini at least 0.94, plausibly approaching 0.99, across the full
creator population.** Reported as a band because grouped public data forces two genuinely
unknowable assumptions — how much the $10M+ tier actually earns, and where mass sits inside the
sub-$1K tier (full accounting in the README) — and the band's low end turns out to be its own
least-defensible corner, not a safe center: a more realistic near-zero assumption for the
sub-$1K tier pushes the estimate toward 0.99, not down. 87.9% of creators earn under $1,000; 116
creators (0.16% of the population) earn over $1 million.

**The attention is worse.** Only 1.18% of all 558,345 published maps have any live players at
all, right now — a full-population statistic that needed no grouping assumption and no
leaderboard truncation to produce, and it is a more extreme concentration signal than the
earnings Gini. Money is unequal; live attention is closer to winner-take-almost-all.

A third finding complicates a naive "just sponsor the leaderboard" strategy: restricted to the
visible top of each leaderboard (Epic Games' own account and modes excluded, since Epic doesn't
compete for its own creator payouts), the top 5 of 29 shown creators hold 45.4% of that group's
lifetime minutes played, and the top 5 of 21 shown maps hold 45.6% of that group's current
players. Those two figures land close enough to look like one shared pattern, but they aren't —
checked at top-3 (28.1% vs. 31.3%) and top-10 (80.9% vs. 72.9%), the two curves diverge by
several points either way, so the ~45% match at exactly the top-5 cut is this snapshot's
coincidence, not a discovered regularity, and isn't treated as one. What does hold at every cut
checked: making the leaderboard doesn't end the inequality — it continues one level in.

**Confidence.** High on the direction of every finding; moderate on the exact earnings Gini,
which is why it's reported as a band rather than a point estimate. This is a single snapshot from
an unofficial, fan-run tracker with no historical API to check it against — it describes one
moment, not a trend, and Epic has never confirmed the site's own methodology. Treat the absolute
levels as approximate and the comparisons — money vs. attention, leaderboard vs. full population —
as the load-bearing part of the analysis.

**Recommendation.** A creator-partnership budget built only around the biggest handful of names
is buying into a pool that is itself unequal at the top — the leaderboard's own top 5 already
captures 45%+ of its combined reach, meaning three or four sponsorship slots are effectively
competing for the same narrow sliver of attention. There is real, underused depth
just below that tier: 2,702 creators (3.6% of the population) sit in the $10K–$300K earnings
range — established enough to have a track record, but well outside the tiny "already-famous"
band most sponsorship searches default to. Because live attention is so scarce platform-wide
(1.18% of maps active at any given moment), a partnership candidate's *current* map traction, not
follower count or historical earnings tier, should be the primary screen before a deal closes — a
creator can carry a large account and have zero live audience today.

**Expected impact:** shifting even 20–30% of a hypothetical top-heavy sponsorship budget into
this mid-tier, traction-screened band should reduce concentration risk — fewer sponsorship
dollars chasing the same narrow top-5 sliver — without materially raising cost per creator,
since this tier sits well below the pricing the top ~116 creators (the $1M+ tiers) can likely
command.
